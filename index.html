One case

BabyBeam: a baby monitor without an account, from first version to 1.5.3.

The problem

A baby monitor is either a separate device you use for two years and then put in a drawer, or an app. The apps out there are expensive as a one-time purchase or ask for a subscription, and often you have to create an account, after which video sits on someone else's servers. I wanted to make something for every parent, without worrying about privacy or cost. I get why it's built that way, but an app that puts a camera in a nursery and then explains why it needs an account has fixed one problem and created another.

Before I wrote the backlog, I listed the complaints about well-known monitors and apps: Nanit, Owlet, Annie, Bibino, Cloud Baby Monitor and Luna (July 2026). Among them: the connection drops silently with no alarm, the sound stops when the screen locks, you can't see the baby phone's battery running down, and core features sit behind a subscription.

The call

Video and audio go straight between the two phones over WebRTC, end-to-end encrypted with DTLS-SRTP. Nothing gets recorded and nothing gets kept, because there's no server to keep it on. The only thing that ever leaves the two phones is a small pairing handshake. That one goes briefly through iCloud, encrypted with a key derived from the pairing code, which I don't have and can't recover.

This still works when you're not home. The two phones find each other over the internet, not just on your own Wi-Fi. What's gone is the account, the subscription, and the company in the middle.

How I prioritised

One principle settled most decisions: a baby monitor that fails silently is worse than a bare-bones one. So everything that builds trust came first: a watchdog that notices a frozen picture, the baby phone's battery on the parent screen, sound that keeps playing with the screen locked, and cry detection on the device itself. Only then comfort, like pairing with a QR code and lullabies from the other room. A task was done only once it also worked on two real iPhones.

Deliberately postponed: several viewers at once, so both parents can watch. Without a server, the baby phone has to send the video separately to each viewer, and that phone is on all night. I decided to build it only after launch, with at most two viewers and the second one audio-only by default. That decision is below, under working notes.

What shipped

  • July 2026First version in the App Store, from 7 July: live video and sound, cry alerts, talk back, QR pairing and status on the lock screen. Free, instead of paid as I'd first planned.
  • AugustA tip jar (1.3). A tip unlocks nothing: putting baby monitor features behind money isn't something I can defend. Then German, French and Spanish, and a feedback button in the app (1.5.0).
  • SeptemberReliability (1.5.1 to 1.5.3): pairing can no longer hang silently, and cry and connection alerts break through the iPhone's Sleep Focus. A request for a tip only appears after six successful sessions and never during use. Feedback can no longer be sent empty.
  • OctoberVersion 1.5.4 in preparation: new screenshots with real video, and a dedicated listing for the UK and Mexico.

What went wrong

The first submission was rejected. Apple's reviewer heard no background audio, even though the app asked for permission to play it. The cause: without two paired devices no session starts, so there's nothing to hear. The quick fix was to drop that permission, but continuous sound with the screen locked is exactly what a baby monitor has to do. I sent a screen recording made on two real devices, with test instructions, and three days later it was approved.

In September, feedback emails started arriving empty: just the template, no message. A German-speaking user had tapped the "translation" category, which sits at the top with German settings, and sent it straight away. The same day I added a required text field, and it went out in 1.5.3.

And the first screenshots were black. The simulator they were made with has no camera, so the most important images of a video baby monitor showed no video. I only found that when I went through the numbers per country.

What I measure

The app sends nothing to me; what I measure comes from App Store Connect. On 4 October 2026, 2.5% of people who saw BabyBeam in the App Store in the US tapped through to its page, against 12.2% in Germany. The black screenshots are the most likely explanation for the US. Version 1.5.4 gets a new set with real video; whether that helps, I'll measure after the release. I also read every feedback email, and the request for a rating only comes after a session of at least ten minutes without connection trouble.

What it cost

  • A setup barrier Both phones need to be signed in to iCloud. Not the same Apple Account, but signed in. Every app with a cloud gets to skip that sentence during setup.
  • Connecting became the user's problem An app that routes everything through its own servers always connects, because there's only one route. Two phones finding each other directly usually works, but sometimes a mobile network gets in the way. Then you have to add a TURN relay yourself, under the gear icon. I moved a type of problem off my plate and onto a settings screen.
  • It reaches the marketing The pairing code is what keeps it secure, so it can't show up anywhere. Not even in my own App Store screenshots, which I had to redo with the code blurred.

Next

Several viewers is the biggest piece still open. It's the heaviest change since the start, and the releases after launch went to reliability, feedback and being found first.

Working notes

Three excerpts from my project notes for BabyBeam. Translated from Dutch and shortened, but not rewritten. I write notes like these together with AI; the decisions in them are mine.

  1. Story · 20 September 2026

    Require a message before the feedback email

    Problem. The feedback flow opens the mail composer straight away, prefilled with just an invitation line and the diagnostics block (version, app language, device, iOS version, role). Nothing forces the user to type their own text above it. Result: emails that look empty — just the template, no message of their own.

    Desired solution.

    1. User picks a category as now.
    2. Instead of starting the composer straight away, the app shows a small text entry screen: "What would you like to report?", explaining that the diagnostics block is added automatically.
    3. Send/next button is disabled while the field is empty (whitespace-only counts as empty).
    4. Only once filled in does the mail composer open, with the user's own message at the top followed by the existing diagnostics block.
    5. "Connection trouble" (no mail flow) stays unchanged.

    Constraints. No new backend. New strings in all 5 languages. Existing unit tests must not break; add tests for the new validation (empty/whitespace-only blocks sending).

    Then. Built the same day and shipped in version 1.5.3.

  2. Decision · 3 July 2026

    Several viewers: only after release

    Context. WebRTC is peer-to-peer and one-to-one. Without an SFU [a server that forwards the video], N viewers means the baby unit maintains N separate peer connections and encodes and uploads the video N times.

    Decision criteria. Principles intact: serverless, no account, nothing stored, reliability first. Battery/heat of the baby unit (which is on all night) manageable.

    Decision. Priority: after the release. First device testing + release with the current features; multi-viewer after that. Max viewers: 2. Extra viewers: 2nd+ audio-only by default, video on request.

    Then. Two technical options worked out, with a preference for the simpler one. Not built yet.

  3. Rejection · 4 July 2026

    App Review, guideline 2.5.4

    Rejected under Guideline 2.5.4: the reviewer heard no audio while UIBackgroundModes: audio is declared. Cause: without two paired devices the reviewer can't start a session, so can't hear any audio either. Do NOT remove the audio mode — continuous background audio (the parent keeps hearing the nursery with the screen locked) is the core feature and exactly the permitted use.

    Then. A screen recording on two real devices, a reply to App Review with test instructions, and the same instructions fixed in the review notes for every later submission. Approved three days later, on 7 July.

On AI

AI writes all the code in these apps; I don't program. My work is everything around it: I decide the problem, the direction and the scope, I set when something is done, I test on real devices and I decide what goes live. That isn't software engineering, and I don't call myself a developer.

What the app does is usually right. It goes wrong in the text around it.

Three examples from this site. All mine, and all live for weeks before I spotted them. The crossed-out line is what the page claimed, underneath is what was actually true:

  • GRID_BREAKER · privacy policy “No network connections, servers, or cloud services.” The game has Game Center leaderboards. The text read well and sounded certain, but it was wrong about my own app, right underneath a live App Store listing.
  • WristVault · landing page “Your collection never leaves your device.” The privacy page right below it described iCloud sync. Both sentences were meant to reassure people, but only one of them was true.
  • WristVault · call to action “Coming soon to the App Store” It was an href="#". The button did nothing at all, and it went live like that.

None of these are bugs. They're claims that sounded right but weren't, in the one spot where only the person who knows the product can check. And I didn't catch them before they went live. I found them weeks later during a review. That changed how I work: text that comes out of AI now gets checked as strictly as the app itself. Nothing goes live until I've held it against what the app actually does, including the legal pages nobody reads.

The other half of the work happens before that. I keep a project structure and a set of written notes I reuse across apps, mostly things I got wrong once and don't want to get wrong again. It means the next app doesn't start from a blank prompt.

How I work

Every app starts with the same question: is this worth solving, and what does a first version actually need to do? I list the complaints about existing apps, check whether those apps actually fix them, then write down what goes in, what comes later and what never goes in. For each task I write down when it's done, and I test on real devices myself.

Scope is only part of it. I also set the price, write the store listing, handle the translations and compliance paperwork, decide what's free and what isn't, and answer the support inbox. Doing all of that myself is the fastest way I know to find out what each choice actually costs.

In the end what matters is how the app feels to use. Something that does less but needs no explaining beats something that does everything and needs a manual.

At work

At Coolblue I work the same way, at a larger scale and with more people. I turn a vision into quarterly roadmaps, prioritise on impact against effort, and bring the interests of commercial, marketing, purchasing and development together. With suppliers I agree on what they'll deliver and hold them to it.

One example: the PlayStation 5 launch in 2020. Very little stock and enormous demand. I worked with development, the app team, marketing and operations at the same time. Afterwards I set up a fixed process for every new batch of stock, so every team knew what had to happen and when. That meant less customer contact. Others built the technology; my part was the coordination and the process.
