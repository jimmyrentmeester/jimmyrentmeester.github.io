One case

BabyBeam: a baby monitor without an account, from first version to 1.5.4.

Problem
Baby monitor apps are expensive or ask for a subscription, and often an account too. I wanted one every parent could use without worrying about privacy or cost.
My main decision
No account and no server of my own. Video and sound go encrypted between two phones, and the app records nothing.
Shipped
Live since 7 July 2026, now version 1.5.4: live video and sound, cry alerts, talk back and QR pairing. After launch, mostly reliability and a better feedback button.
What I see and don't know yet
Few people in the US tap through to the page in the App Store. My guess is that the black screenshots play a part. Version 1.5.4 has a new set; whether it helps, I'll know in a few weeks. How many people actually use the app, I don't know.
What I learned
Reliability first, extra features later. And whatever is claimed about the app, including on the App Store page, I check as strictly as the app itself.

The problem

There are standalone baby monitors, which end up in a drawer after a few years, and there are apps. Those apps are expensive if you buy them outright, or you're tied to a subscription. Often you also have to create an account. I wanted a baby monitor without an account and without stored video, one every parent could use without worrying about privacy or cost.

There was already a first working version: two phones that pair with a code and pass on video and sound. Before I wrote a backlog for it, I collected the complaints about well-known monitors and apps: Nanit, Owlet, Annie, Bibino, Cloud Baby Monitor and Luna (early July 2026). Among other things, people complained that the connection drops with no alarm, that the sound stops when the screen locks, that you can't see the baby phone's battery running down, and that basic features sit behind a subscription.

The call

I don't run a server of my own. Video and audio go over WebRTC between the two phones, encrypted with DTLS-SRTP, and the app records nothing. For pairing, the app uses Apple's iCloud: both phones leave a small message there, encrypted with a key derived from the pairing code. That code only lives on the two phones. Once the connection is up, each phone deletes its own message again.

It usually works when you're not home too. The phones look for each other over the internet and, to do that, ask a Google STUN server for their internet address. No video or sound passes through it.

How I prioritised

A baby monitor that stops without warning is worse than one with few features. That was my starting point. So everything that builds trust came first: a watchdog that notices when the picture freezes, the baby phone's battery on the parent screen, sound that keeps playing when the screen locks, and cry detection on the device itself. Only after that came things like QR pairing and lullabies from the other room. Building went fast: AI built phases 0 to 3 on 2 and 3 July. I ticked off a task once its tests passed, and then tested everything on two real iPhones.

I deliberately postponed several viewers at once, so both parents can watch. Without a server, the baby phone has to send the video separately to each viewer, and that phone is on all night. I decided to pick this up only after launch, with at most two viewers and the second one audio-only by default. The decision itself is below, under working notes.

What shipped

  • July 2026First version in the App Store, live on 7 July. With live video and sound, cry alerts, talk back, QR pairing and status on the lock screen. I had first planned it as a paid app, but it went live for free.
  • AugustA tip jar (1.3). A tip doesn't buy anything extra; putting baby monitor features behind money isn't something I can defend. After that came German, French and Spanish, and a feedback button in the app (1.5.0).
  • SeptemberMostly reliability (1.5.1 to 1.5.3). Pairing can no longer hang without you noticing, and cry and connection alerts get through the iPhone's Sleep Focus. The app only asks for a tip after six successful sessions, never during use. And you can no longer send an empty feedback email.
  • OctoberVersion 1.5.4, live on 5 October: new screenshots and a preview video, and a dedicated listing for the UK and Mexico. The simulator has no camera, so the monitor in them plays a stock video of a baby.

What went wrong

My first submission was rejected on 4 July. Apple's reviewer heard no background audio, even though the app asked for permission to play it. That was because no session starts without two paired devices, so there was nothing to hear. I could have dropped that permission, but continuous sound with the screen locked is exactly what a baby monitor is for. I sent a screen recording made on two real devices, with test instructions. Three days later the app was approved.

In September I got feedback emails with no message, just the template. A German-speaking user had tapped the "translation" category, which sits at the top with German settings, and sent the email straight away. That same day I had a required text field added. It went out in 1.5.3.

The first screenshots turned out to be black. The simulator they were made with has no camera, so the most important images of a video baby monitor showed no video. I only found out when I looked at the numbers per country.

What I measure

The app sends me nothing. What I measure comes from App Store Connect. From 7 July to 4 October 2026, BabyBeam was shown 5,579 times in the App Store in the US, and its product page was opened 140 times after that: 2.5%. In Germany it was 68 out of 558, 12.2%. My guess is that the black screenshots play a part, but a difference between countries doesn't prove that. The new set has been live since version 1.5.4; whether it helps remains to be seen. These numbers say nothing about how many people actually use the app. I also read every feedback email. The app only asks for a rating after a session of at least ten minutes without connection trouble.

What it cost
  • An extra step during setup Both phones need to be signed in to iCloud. Not with the same Apple Account, but signed in. An app with its own cloud doesn't have to ask for that.
  • Connecting is partly up to the user Two phones looking for each other directly usually find each other, but sometimes a provider's network blocks that. Then you have to add a TURN relay yourself, in the settings. Video and sound then go through that relay, still encrypted. In effect, I've handed that problem to the user.
  • The marketing had to change too The pairing code keeps the connection secure, so it can't be visible anywhere. Not even on my own App Store screenshots. I had to redo those with the code unreadable.

Next

Several viewers is the biggest piece still open, and also the heaviest change since the start. After launch, reliability, feedback and discoverability came first.

  2. Story · 20 September 2026

    Require a message before the feedback email

    Then. Built the same day and shipped in version 1.5.3. Read the excerpt

    Problem. The feedback flow opens the mail composer straight away, prefilled with just an invitation line and the diagnostics block (version, app language, device, iOS version, role). Nothing forces the user to type their own text above it. Result: emails that look empty — just the template, no message of their own.

    Desired solution.

    1. User picks a category as now.
    2. Instead of starting the composer straight away, the app shows a small text entry screen: "What would you like to report?", explaining that the diagnostics block is added automatically.
    3. Send/next button is disabled while the field is empty (whitespace-only counts as empty).
    4. Only once filled in does the mail composer open, with the user's own message at the top followed by the existing diagnostics block.
    5. "Connection trouble" (no mail flow) stays unchanged.

    Constraints. No new backend. New strings in all 5 languages. Existing unit tests must not break; add tests for the new validation (empty/whitespace-only blocks sending).

  3. Decision · 3 July 2026

    Several viewers: only after release

    Then. Two technical options worked out, with a preference for the simpler one. Not built yet. Read the excerpt

    Context. WebRTC is peer-to-peer and one-to-one. Without an SFU [a server that forwards the video], N viewers means the baby unit maintains N separate peer connections and encodes and uploads the video N times.

    Decision criteria. Principles intact: serverless, no account, nothing stored, reliability first. Battery/heat of the baby unit (which is on all night) manageable.

    Decision. Priority: after the release. First device testing + release with the current features; multi-viewer after that. Max viewers: 2. Extra viewers: 2nd+ audio-only by default, video on request.

  4. Rejection · 4 July 2026

    App Review, guideline 2.5.4

    Then. A screen recording on two real devices, a reply to App Review with test instructions, and the same instructions fixed in the review notes for every later submission. Approved three days later, on 7 July. Read the excerpt

    Rejected under Guideline 2.5.4: the reviewer heard no audio while UIBackgroundModes: audio is declared. Cause: without two paired devices the reviewer can't start a session, so can't hear any audio either. Do NOT remove the audio mode — continuous background audio (the parent keeps hearing the nursery with the screen locked) is the core feature and exactly the permitted use.

On AI

AI writes all the code in these apps. I don't program myself. My job is deciding which problem I solve, what goes in and when something is done. I test on real devices and I decide what goes live. So I don't call myself a developer.

What the app does is usually right. It goes wrong in the text around it.

Three examples from this site. All three were online for weeks before I noticed. Crossed out is what the page claimed, underneath is what was actually true.

  • GRID_BREAKER · privacy policy “No network connections, servers, or cloud services.” The game has Game Center leaderboards. The sentence sounded convincing but wasn't true, right under a live App Store listing.
  • WristVault · landing page “Your collection never leaves your device.” The privacy page below it actually described iCloud sync. Both sentences were meant to reassure people, but only the second was true.
  • WristVault · call to action “Coming soon to the App Store” It was an href="#". The button did nothing, and it went live like that.

These aren't bugs, but sentences that sounded right and weren't. Only someone who knows the product can check that. I found them weeks later, during a review. Since then I check text from AI as strictly as the app itself. Nothing goes live until I've held it against the product, the legal pages included.

I work with a fixed project structure and notes I reuse for each app. They mostly record what went wrong before, so I don't do it again. A new app doesn't start from zero because of that.
